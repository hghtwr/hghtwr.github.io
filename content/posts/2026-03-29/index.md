I'm not an AI power-user. I'm also not an AI naysayer. I consider myself a hands-on bystander. 
I'm touched by AI from both its extremes, as a contributor but also as a responsible for my team's outcomes and delivery performance. 

So, maybe call me an engaged side-liner. Like a football coach presented with a new high-tech ball, I stand at the side-line, carefully eagle-eyeing it, kicking it left, right, reading about it.
And then I realize, this ball is different. Different rules of physics apply to this ball. So, I start wondering if this new ball might be what wins us the championship or the end of this beautiful sport whatsoever.
And while I think, all my players and all other players in the world, start kicking and testing the same ball...

## War never changes...

The first thing, I (probably everyone) notice is that this is nothing new.
When I see how Software Engineers are right now lining themselves up on a spectrum of factions, I instinctively have to think about a piece in RandsInRepose I read about a while ago. It was about "Volatiles and Stables" (https://randsinrepose.com/archives/stables-and-volatiles/). 
We have those, who want to build software. They use AI in various degrees and the most important part is that it's getting the job done. Speed and impact is perceived as value and (not necessarily rightfully) matched to AI's skills to rapidly iterate on a proposal and implement it. 
Their perfect scenario is running at least a couple of agents at the same time, each implementing a feature at the same time. 

The others, they want to write software. They want to hand-craft every line of code and that's exactly what's the fun for them. They rely on craftsmanship, planning and detail-oriented excellence in execution. 
The fun is in the process, putting all their passion into the process like a handcrafting woodworker that cannot understand why someone else is using a CNC machine. They have built the most amazing things in the past but now they feel like losing what they really care about. 

Both are right. Both fight each other. 

And then we have managers. I'm not talking about those, that mandate AI usage by making it part of the performance review. I talk about those, that actually care about humans. 
As if managing a team wasn't difficult enough, AI opens a new battlefield in an old war. Moving from managing scarcity ("We don't have enough people to do this") to managing abundance ("We don't have enough people to review/operate/maintain/yadayadayada this") feels like a fundamental shift in paradigm. 

On a side note, everyone's kind of a manager now but for their agents, multiple at a time: You carefully check all your communication for every word used to make sure you don't give a false impression? Double-reading the ideas you have written down, thinking about the right amount of context to convey what you want to build?  Arguing with yourself if you should have prioritized another thing first but now it's too late and half the work is already done? 

Sounds a lot like management to me. 

I can feel it myself. Suddenly, I think about building things I would have dismissed half a year ago. Not because I thought it was too hard to build but because I was afraid we had to maintain it. But damn, being able to build it so much faster now makes it just so much more compelling. 

The seduction is real and I can see both sides. I could just throw more software at our problems. More software will fix it. After all, both factions have something in common: they like to **create** something. That's why we picked this up, right? 
You were faced with some challenge to make this machine made of electric circuits do beep-boop-bing. You pressed the right buttons and it made beep-boop-bing. You got hooked on solving puzzle after puzzle on how to get it to make more sophisticated things and you ended up here. 

But how to balance it out? Sometimes more software **will** fix it, after all. Everything is a trade-off. And it's not just speed versus quality. 

If you think trying comes with no costs, you are wrong. 

Shipping a feature versus burning out one of your Stables. Building fast versus feeding the biggest intellectual property heist in history. Hitting your OKRs versus gutting the pipeline of future Senior Engineers. Moving quickly versus never giving anyone the chance to learn slowly. Launching another agent while California is still burning...

> I'm deeply afraid the fear of missing out will make me buy into something I'll regret and equally afraid that not buying in is what I'll regret most.

Settling the war is the same job as always: Talk to people, balance it out. After all, you are the leader. 
I'll choose my way to be wrong in this. 

But God, I will hate the next couple of years...
